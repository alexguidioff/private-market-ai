## The 6-block structure (from Maria Scetta's template)

1. **Greeting**
2. *"I am writing to enquire about…"* — the ask, in one line
3. **What struck you about their research** (specific, shows you read them)
4. **How your research connects to theirs** (the bridge)
5. **Who you are** (credentials, in 3–4 lines)
6. **Attachments + the key closing sentence** (collaboration + funded positions)

Keep it to ~200–250 words. The professor decides in the first three lines whether to keep reading.

## The one sentence you must keep (verbatim shape)
> *"I would be very grateful for the opportunity to discuss whether there is scope for a doctoral
> collaboration, and whether there are funded positions or doctoral assistantships within your group
> for [year]."*

It does two jobs at once: signals you want a real collaboration (not just advice) **and** surfaces
whether money exists — without you having to ask "will you pay me?" directly.

## On funding: the professor can be your *garante* (sponsor)
A professor doesn't only supervise — they can act as the **academic host / applicant** for funding
that a student alone cannot access. Worth knowing before the call:

- Many PhD positions are simply **funded from the professor's existing grant** (SNSF project /
  ERC / Innosuisse). This is the most common route — hence the "funded positions" phrasing above.
- **SNSF** has doctoral-level instruments where the **professor is the applicant/host** and the
  PhD salary sits inside the grant. There are also mobility/return instruments on the researcher
  side. ⚠️ Program names, eligibility, and amounts change — **verify on snf.ch before citing any of
  this to a professor.** Do not name a specific scheme unless you've checked it.
- **BRIDGE (SNSF + Innosuisse)** and **Innosuisse Innovation Projects** need the professor as the
  academic host too (see `Funding.md`).

**How to raise it in the call (not the first email):** *"If there's no funded seat right now, would
you be open to jointly applying — e.g. as academic host — to an SNSF/BRIDGE line for a position on
this topic?"* This reframes you from "cost" to "someone who brings a fundable project."

## The referral move (when a professor says no)
A "no" is still worth a connection. Always close a declined thread with:

> *"Thank you for the honest answer. Since you know this space far better than I do — is there a
> colleague, in Switzerland or elsewhere, whose work on `[[topic]]` you think this project would fit
> better? I'd be grateful for any pointer."*

Most professors are happy to redirect, and a **warm referral** ("Prof. X suggested I write to you")
has a far higher reply rate than a cold email. This is how you turn rejections into a growing network.
