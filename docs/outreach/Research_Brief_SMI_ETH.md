# AI-Supported Information Acquisition for Strategic Decisions under Uncertainty

**Research brief for ETH Zurich — Strategic Management and Innovation**  
**Alessandro Guidi** · alexguidioff@gmail.com · alessandroguidi.site · linkedin.com/in/alessandroguidi1 · github.com/alexguidioff

## Motivation and research question

Strategic investment decisions are made with incomplete evidence, limited attention, and costly opportunities to learn more. AI is commonly used to predict outcomes or summarise available information; a prior organizational question is **how AI changes what decision-makers choose to investigate, when they escalate or stop, and how judgment is allocated between people and machines**.

> **How does AI-supported information acquisition reshape organizational attention and decision processes under uncertainty—and when does it improve decision quality rather than merely prediction accuracy?**

Private-market investment provides a demanding empirical setting: evidence is fragmented, diligence is costly, outcomes are delayed, and investment teams must decide whether to continue investigating or stop. The proposed unit of analysis is a decision at a fixed time, represented by the information defensibly available then. An AI system may recommend acquiring an information block—or no additional information—subject to its cost and delay:  
`information state → acquire / do not acquire → updated state → continue diligence / stop`.

## Foundation

My study *Can Non-Financial Signals Price Private Companies? A Machine Learning Approach to Startup Valuation* (with S. Rashid and H. Zhong; accepted, **WI2026 Student Track**) analyses **3,403 PitchBook deals** under a strict out-of-time holdout across the 2022 regime shift. In that study, non-financial signals priced private companies competitively with the strongest financial baseline; within the tested models, investor-syndicate capacity was the most influential signal. This result motivates moving beyond outcome prediction toward the design and evaluation of information-acquisition processes.

## Proposed empirical programme

1. **Reconstruct point-in-time information states.** Use dated public evidence to represent what could defensibly have been observed at each decision time, explicitly separating later outcome evidence from contemporaneously available information.
2. **Evaluate cost-aware acquisition policies.** Compare AI-supported recommendations with preregistered non-VoI baselines on decision value net of information cost and delay—not prediction accuracy alone. A synthetic multi-world branch can test recovery and counterfactual logic but will not be treated as evidence of market realism.
3. **Study organizational mechanisms.** Through controlled experiments and, if available, a field or archival study with an investment team, examine effects on attention allocation, escalation and stopping, confidence, overrides, and the division of judgment between humans and AI. An industry partner would strengthen external validity but is not required for initial method development.

## Feasibility and evidence boundary

An initial public-data pipeline uses US technology-related primary issuers with Form D anchors; its current outcome is an explicitly weak proxy, not a Series A, priced round, or success label. A **20-case AI-assisted double-review feasibility pilot** obtained 75% raw exact agreement, while 45% of cases were initially unknown or unresolved. It supports protocol development and sensitivity analysis only: it is not human-adjudicated gold data, does not estimate prevalence, and does not demonstrate that a value-of-information policy works. Stronger empirical claims would require independent human adjudication or partner data.

## Expected contribution and fit with SMI

The intended contribution is organizational rather than a private-market prediction tool: a process account of how AI-mediated information acquisition redistributes attention, structures strategy creation, changes escalation and stopping, and reallocates judgment in consequential decisions. Private markets serve as the empirical setting through which these mechanisms can be observed and tested.

This direction fits SMI’s interest in digital technology and AI as tools and agents in strategy creation, organizational adaptation, and knowledge processes. My background combines entrepreneurial-finance research, applied machine learning, product management, and cross-functional organizational work; I seek supervision to deepen the organizational theory, research design, and decision-theoretic foundations.
