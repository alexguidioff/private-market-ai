The Value of Information in Private-Market Investment Decisions
Alessandro Guidi  ·  alexguidioff@gmail.com  ·  alessandroguidi.site  ·  linkedin.com/in/alessandroguidi1  ·  github.com/alexguidioff

Target institution

University of St. Gallen — Chair of Private Markets & Alternative Investments, School of Finance (Prof. Tereza Tykvová), Swiss Finance Institute. A methodological co-advisor at ETH (decision-making under uncertainty / machine learning) is envisaged for the modelling side.

1.  Research context and focus

Investors in private markets decide under extreme uncertainty on fragmentary, non-standardised information. Much empirical work — including your own — establishes what drives private-market outcomes (syndication, selection, the risk profile of buyouts). The question I want to open sits one step earlier and is, so far, largely unasked: given a decision, which missing piece of information — at what cost — most improves it? Not which factor predicts success, but which piece of diligence is worth acquiring.

This builds on my accepted WI2026 Student Track paper (Can Non-Financial Signals Price Private Companies?), which shows on 3,403 PitchBook deals, under a strict out-of-time holdout, that non-financial signals price private companies competitively with financial baselines — and that investor-syndicate structure, not firm financials, is the dominant driver. That last finding connects directly to your work on private-equity and venture syndication, and it exposes the ceiling of a purely predictive approach.

2.  Research questions

Main question: In private-market investment decisions, which information acquisition — at what cost — most improves the quality of the decision?

  • How can each decision be represented by its information state: what is knowable at time t, reconstructed point-in-time, before the outcome is realised?
  • How do we quantify the marginal value of an additional piece of information relative to its cost?
  • Does the value of information differ across settings — venture vs. buyout, syndicated vs. solo, primary vs. secondary deals?

3.  Theoretical framework

The project brings the economics of value of information (Howard; Savage) into private-market investment decisions. Your empirical private-markets programme identifies the factors that matter for outcomes; value-of-information theory asks the normative counterpart — what is worth knowing next, and whether it is worth its cost. The core object is the expected improvement in decision quality from moving Decision(state) → acquire info (cost c) → state' → Decision(state'). The technical machinery (Bayesian experimental design; sequential decision-making under uncertainty) exists in the ML literature; its application to private-market diligence is open, and it needs a rigorous empirical-finance anchor to be posed and tested credibly.

4.  Methodology

Feasibility does not depend on proprietary data. Ground truth comes from a point-in-time reconstruction of public sources (so the information state at time t excludes look-ahead — an identification strategy familiar from empirical finance) plus a virtual-fund simulator that measures decision quality counterfactually. On top of this I estimate the value of each information type and rank diligence steps by expected value per unit cost, benchmarked against the WI2026 pipeline as a predictive baseline.

  • Year 1: formalise the information-state / value-of-information framing; build the point-in-time dataset and simulator.
  • Year 2: estimate information value across settings; complete and submit the flagship paper.
  • Year 3–4: extensions (syndication signals, buyout vs. venture), writing, publications.

5.  Expected contribution

The project contributes (i) a value-of-information framing for private-market decisions — a normative account of which signals are worth acquiring; (ii) a reproducible point-in-time and simulator methodology that makes the question testable without proprietary data; and (iii) empirical evidence on where diligence effort is mis-allocated across deal types. It speaks directly to entrepreneurial-finance and private-equity audiences and generalises across the alternative- investments spectrum, which makes it a research line rather than a single study.

Your survey of the VC/PE literature and its research agenda maps the very gaps a value-of-information perspective can address, and your work on syndication connects to my paper's central finding.
